(2bi)
(i) Formation: Sedimentary rocks are formed through the deposition, compaction and cementation of sediments, while metamorphic rocks are formed when existing rocks are changed by heat and pressure.

(ii) Structure: Sedimentary rocks usually occur in layers or strata, while metamorphic rocks may have a banded, foliated or crystalline structure.

(iii) Fossils: Sedimentary rocks may contain fossils of plants and animals, while metamorphic rocks rarely contain fossils because heat and pressure usually destroy them.

(iv) Hardness: Sedimentary rocks are generally softer and less compact, while metamorphic rocks are usually harder, denser and more compact.

(2bii)
(i) Source of Minerals: Rocks provide valuable minerals such as gold, iron, tin and limestone, which are used in industries and manufacturing.

(ii) Construction Materials: Rocks are used for building houses, roads, bridges and other structures. Examples include granite, marble and sandstone.

(iii) Source of Fuel: Some sedimentary rocks contain fossil fuels such as coal and petroleum, which are important sources of energy for domestic and industrial activities.

(4a)
(i) Latitude: Places near the equator receive more direct sunlight and are generally hotter, while places farther from the equator are usually cooler.
(ii) Altitude: Areas at high altitudes experience lower temperatures than areas at low altitudes because temperature decreases with increasing height above sea level.
(iii) Distance from the Sea: Coastal areas usually experience moderate temperatures because the sea heats up and cools down slowly, while inland areas often experience greater temperature variations.
(iv) Prevailing Winds: Winds influence climate by carrying heat and moisture from one region to another. Winds blowing from the sea usually bring moisture and rainfall, while dry continental winds may bring little or no rainfall.

(4bi)
Rainfall is measured using a rain gauge.

(i) The rain gauge consists of a funnel, collecting container and graduated measuring cylinder.
(ii) It is placed in an open area away from trees and buildings.
(iii) Rainwater collected in the container is poured into a graduated measuring cylinder.
(iv) The amount of rainfall is read and recorded in millimetres (mm).

(4bii)
Temperature is measured using a thermometer, usually a maximum and minimum thermometer.

(i) The thermometer is placed in a Stevenson screen to protect it from direct sunlight and rainfall.
(ii) The maximum thermometer records the highest temperature, while the minimum thermometer records the lowest temperature within a given period.
(iii) The readings are taken from the thermometers at the appropriate observation time.
(iv) Temperature is recorded in degrees Celsius (°C).

(4biii)
Relative humidity is measured using a hygrometer, particularly a wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer (psychrometer).

(i) The instrument consists of two thermometers: a dry-bulb thermometer and a wet-bulb thermometer.
(ii) The wet-bulb thermometer is covered with a moist cloth, while the dry-bulb thermometer remains uncovered.
(iii) Water evaporating from the wet cloth cools the wet-bulb thermometer, producing a difference between the two thermometer readings.
(iv) The difference in readings is used with a relative humidity table to determine the relative humidity, expressed as a percentage (%).

Remote sensing is the process of collecting information about the earth's surface and features from a distance without making direct physical contact with them, usually through satellites, aircraft and sensors.

(5aii)

(ii) Agricultural Monitoring: It helps farmers and agricultural experts to monitor crop growth, identify diseased crops and assess soil conditions.
(iii) Environmental Monitoring: It helps to detect environmental changes such as deforestation, desertification, flooding and pollution.

(i) Global Positioning: Global positioning refers to the process of determining the exact location or position of an object or place on the earth's surface using a Global Positioning System (GPS). It uses signals received from satellites to determine the latitude, longitude and altitude of a location.

(ii) Attributes: Attributes are descriptive information or characteristics attached to geographical features in a Geographic Information System (GIS). They provide details about features such as the name, size, population, height and type of a particular location or object.

(iii) Georeferencing: Georeferencing is the process of assigning real-world geographical coordinates to a map, image or aerial photograph so that it can be correctly positioned on the earth's surface. It allows different geographical data to be accurately compared and combined.

(iv) Digitizing: Digitizing is the process of converting geographical information from paper maps, photographs or other analogue sources into digital format for storage, analysis and display in a computer-based GIS. It involves tracing features such as roads, rivers and boundaries.

(v) Map Layer: A map layer is a collection of related geographical information displayed together on a map in a GIS. Each layer represents a particular feature, such as roads, rivers, buildings, vegetation or administrative boundaries.
